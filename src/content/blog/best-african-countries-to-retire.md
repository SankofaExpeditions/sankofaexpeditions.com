## What Makes a Country Actually Good for Retirement

Before the country rankings, here is how we evaluated each place. These are the criteria that determine whether you can actually *live* there, not just visit.

**1. Retirement Visa or Residency Pathway**
Does the country have a formal program for retirees? If not, what is the best legal pathway for long-term residency? Can you stay without anchoring yourself to a job or investment?

**2. Healthcare Infrastructure**
Private hospital access, medical evacuation logistics, international health insurance acceptance. We evaluated private healthcare specifically, because public healthcare in most of these countries is underfunded and not the right answer for retirees managing chronic conditions.

**3. Cost of Living at Retirement Standard**
Not survival-level. A comfortable diaspora retirement lifestyle: quality apartment in a good neighborhood, groceries, transportation, utilities, restaurant meals, and healthcare. We used real cost data, not aspirational estimates.

**4. Safety and Stability**
Political stability and day-to-day crime. We looked at both. A country can be politically stable and still have neighborhood-level crime that changes your daily life. We name the specific cities and areas that work, not just the country overall.

**5. Practical Infrastructure**
Reliable electricity, water, internet, banking, and international flight connections. These matter more in retirement than in a working-age relocation because you are less likely to absorb friction with energy and urgency.

**6. Diaspora Community**
Is there an existing community of returnees and expats? This matters for social integration, for navigating local bureaucracy, and for not feeling like you landed somewhere alone.

**7. Flight Connections Home**
You will want to visit family. Medical emergencies happen. How easy is it to get back to the US, UK, or Canada when you need to?

Each country is scored 1-10 on these criteria. The ranking reflects the total picture, weighted toward what matters most in retirement.

No country in Africa has built a more intentional welcome for diaspora returnees than Ghana. The Year of Return was not just a tourism campaign. It seeded a community that has been growing ever since.

**The Right of Abode**

If you have African heritage, Ghana is offering you something no country in the Western world ever has: the right to come home and stay. The Right of Abode under Ghana's Immigration Act grants indefinite residency to anyone of African descent. It allows you to live, work, invest, buy property, and move freely in Ghana without a visa or work permit.

Application cost: approximately $100.

26 years this pathway has existed.

The administrative processing has seen reported disruptions and backlogs in 2026, based on diaspora community reports. The legal framework is intact. If you are applying, do it through the Ghana Passport and Citizenship Office or via your nearest Ghana Embassy, and build in timeline buffer.

**Where to Actually Live**

Do not retire to Accra proper. The traffic alone will age you. The retirement-ready communities are east of the city:

- **East Legon:** Established, secure, well-serviced. Home to the largest concentration of diaspora returnees. Quiet residential streets, good restaurants, reliable power (generator backup is standard in this bracket).
- **Trasacco Valley / Cantonments area:** More upscale. American School nearby. More expatriate infrastructure.
- **Airport Residential:** Good central location, less congested than the city core.

If you want lower cost and slower pace, **Cape Coast** (3 hours west) offers beachside living at a fraction of Accra prices and is home to Cape Coast Castle, one of the most significant ancestral sites on the continent.

**Healthcare**

The private system is what matters: Trust Hospital, Lister Hospital, and the University of Ghana Medical Centre all handle routine and moderate-complexity care well. For cardiac surgery, oncology, or orthopedic procedures, plan for medical evacuation to South Africa or Europe. Medical evacuation insurance is essential.

**The Ghana Argument**

For African American, British-African, and Caribbean diaspora retirees, Ghana has something the other eight countries on this list cannot offer: homecoming. The community is built. The events are happening. The language gap is small or nonexistent. The cultural infrastructure is there.

## 2. South Africa: The City Retirement Option

**Overall Score: 8.5/10**

| Criterion | Score | Notes |
|-----------|-------|-------|
| Retirement Visa | 8/10 | Formal Retirement Annuity Visa, 4-year renewable |
| Healthcare | 10/10 | World-class private system in Cape Town, Johannesburg |
| Cost of Living | 7/10 | Cape Town: $2,000-$3,500/month |
| Safety | 5/10 | Cape Town is manageable, Johannesburg needs adaptation |
| Infrastructure | 9/10 | First-world infrastructure in major cities |
| Diaspora Community | 8/10 | Growing Black diaspora community in Cape Town |
| Flight Connections | 9/10 | OR Tambo (JHB) is Africa's primary international hub |

South Africa gets a split verdict. The country has the continent's best private healthcare, its most internationally cosmopolitan cities, world-class wine country, and infrastructure that is genuinely comparable to Western Europe in certain neighborhoods. It also has a crime problem that requires a different kind of daily awareness than most Western retirees are accustomed to.

The answer is not to avoid South Africa. The answer is to be specific about where within South Africa you are going.

**The Retirement Annuity Visa**

South Africa has a formal retirement visa for foreigners who can demonstrate pension or annuity income. Requirements as of our research:

- Minimum monthly income of approximately ZAR 37,000 (roughly USD $2,000 at current exchange rates; verify current rand rate before applying)
- Proof of pension, annuity, or retirement fund income
- Medical fitness certificate
- Police clearance certificate from your home country
- 4-year initial permit, renewable
- Cannot take employment while on this visa

South Africa's rand has weakened significantly against major currencies over the past decade. For US dollar pensioners and Social Security recipients, this translates directly into purchasing power that stretches. A $2,500 monthly income that feels modest in the United States goes substantially further in Cape Town.

**Where in South Africa**

**Cape Town** is the tier-one option. The Atlantic Seaboard neighborhoods (Sea Point, Green Point, De Waterkant) and the Southern Suburbs (Claremont, Constantia) are where internationally-minded retirees build community. These neighborhoods have:

- 24/7 security infrastructure as a standard feature of residential properties
- Private healthcare accessible within 10 minutes
- Excellent restaurant and cultural life
- Moderate climate year-round (no extreme heat or cold)
- One of the most beautiful natural settings of any city on earth

**Johannesburg** is for people with business reasons to be there. The lifestyle is good in the northern suburbs (Sandton, Rosebank, Melrose) but the city-wide crime rate is higher and the daily security calculus is more demanding.

**The Garden Route** (George, Knysna, Plettenberg Bay) is worth serious consideration for retirees who want lower cost, slower pace, and extraordinary natural beauty.

**Healthcare**

Cape Town's private healthcare system is the best on the continent. Netcare, Life Healthcare, and Mediclinic operate hospitals with internationally trained specialists. Cardiac procedures, cancer treatment, orthopedic surgery: all of these can be handled in Cape Town at a level that meets international standards.

Rwanda is the only country on this list that keeps surprising people. Travelers who arrive expecting a developing-world experience find Kigali: a city that is cleaner, safer, and better-organized than many major Western capitals. Single-use plastics are banned. Streets are swept daily. Corruption prosecutions are public and real.

**The Retirement Pathway**

Rwanda offers a 5-year Special Residence Permit that covers retirees and other long-term residents. Requirements include proof of financial stability and health insurance. Contact the Directorate General of Immigration and Emigration in Kigali for current fee schedules and documentation requirements.

**Kigali as a City**

The Nyarutarama and Kacyiru neighborhoods are where most international residents live. Quality apartments, good grocery options, reliable internet, 20 minutes from the international airport.

The Global Peace Index consistently ranks Rwanda among the continent's safest. That is not marketing. It is what decades of intentional governance look like.

**Healthcare**

Kigali's healthcare is Rwanda's major ongoing investment. The King Faisal Hospital handles moderate-complexity cases. For anything requiring advanced cardiac, oncology, or orthopedic procedures, medical evacuation to Nairobi or Johannesburg is standard. Rwanda has been building toward becoming a regional medical hub as part of its Vision 2050 plan. That infrastructure is coming.

Tanzania does not make most retirement shortlists. Zanzibar specifically should.

The island sits off the Tanzanian coast in the Indian Ocean, but culturally and geographically it is Swahili East Africa, not an isolated island economy. It is culturally distinct and deeply connected to continental African history. The Stone Town UNESCO World Heritage Site is one of the most significant trade and diaspora crossroads in African history. The white sand beaches are genuinely world-class. The cost of living is among the lowest on this list.

**The Visa Pathway**

Tanzania offers a retirement residence permit for people over 50. Requirements include proof of a fixed monthly income (verify current minimums with the Immigration Department of Tanzania). Note: Tanzania does not permit dual citizenship, so if citizenship by descent is part of your longer-term plan, understand what you are working toward before applying for permanent status.

**Zanzibar: Stone Town vs. the North Coast**

**Stone Town** is where history lives. The labyrinthine old city is a UNESCO site, the architecture is Arab-Swahili, and the food is extraordinary. Living here means being embedded in a real city with real texture.

**Nungwi and Kendwa** on the north coast are where the beach retirement picture comes from. Calm turquoise water, quality resorts that have driven residential infrastructure, growing expat community. More expensive than Stone Town but still far cheaper than comparable beach destinations globally.

**Healthcare**

This is the honest constraint on Zanzibar. The island has basic private clinics for routine care. Serious conditions route to Dar es Salaam (short flight) or medical evacuation to Nairobi or South Africa. If you have complex medical needs, the healthcare gap matters. If you are healthy and looking for a low-cost beach retirement in a culturally rich setting, Zanzibar works.

## 5. Botswana: The Most Underrated Option

**Overall Score: 7.8/10**

| Healthcare | 7/10 | Gaborone private solid; complex care in South Africa |
| Cost of Living | 8/10 | $1,500-$2,500/month in Gaborone |
| Safety | 9/10 | Among Africa's safest; consistently top-ranked |
| Infrastructure | 8/10 | Well-maintained for the region |

| Flight Connections | 7/10 | Gaborone to Johannesburg, then connect |

Botswana does not appear on retirement shortlists. It should be near the top.

The country has run a functioning multiparty democracy since independence in 1966. That is not a claim most countries in any region can make. It has the continent's longest streak of democratic governance, transparent elections, and clean governance metrics. The rule of law functions. Property rights for foreigners are respected. Contracts hold.

That track record matters for retirement planning in a way that is hard to quantify but easy to feel once you understand what its absence costs you elsewhere.

**The Retirement Permit**

Botswana offers a formal Retirement Permit for people over 55 (verify current age threshold with the Department of Immigration). Requirements include proof of pension or retirement income, typically around $1,500/month, health insurance, and a police clearance certificate. The permit is renewable and allows you to live in Botswana without working.

**Gaborone and Beyond**

**Gaborone** is a small capital by African standards, around 400,000 people. It is clean, well-organized, and has solid private healthcare infrastructure (Bokamoso Private Hospital is the flagship). The Phakalane and Game City neighborhoods are where international residents cluster.

What Gaborone lacks in size it makes up in calm. This is a city where things work. Streets are maintained. Electricity is reliable. The banking system processes international transfers without drama.

**The Okavango Option**

For retirees who want the bush, the Maun gateway town to the Okavango Delta offers a genuinely extraordinary natural setting. Smaller, more remote, and cheaper than Gaborone. Medical care routes to Gaborone (1-hour flight). It is not for everyone. For the right person, there is nothing like it on earth.

**Healthcare**

Gaborone's Bokamoso Private Hospital handles routine and moderate-complexity care. South Africa's hospitals are three hours by road or a short flight from Gaborone, making this one of the most accessible evacuation situations on the continent. Medical evacuation insurance is still recommended.

Dakar is underrated on the global retirement circuit, and that is partly why it still works financially.

Senegal operates on West African CFA francs, pegged to the euro. That peg gives you stability (no currency collapse risk like the naira), but it means Dakar costs more than other Francophone West African cities. It is still substantially cheaper than Accra at current exchange rates, and dramatically cheaper than European alternatives with similar quality of life.

**The Visa Situation**

Senegal does not have a dedicated retirement visa program as of this writing. Americans can enter visa-free and stay for up to 90 days. After that, the pathway is a Carte de Séjour obtained through the Direction des Étrangers et des Titres de Voyage. It is a bureaucratic process, navigable with patience or local help.

**Dakar Neighborhoods**

- **Les Almadies:** Upscale peninsula northwest of the city. Embassies, international restaurants, diplomatic community. High-quality infrastructure. The ocean is nearby.
- **Plateau:** The historic center. Walkable, cultural, central.
- **Mermoz / Sacré-Coeur:** Strong Senegalese professional middle class, good grocery access, calmer than central Dakar.

Abidjan does not get enough credit in English-language retirement content because most of that content is written in English, and Côte d'Ivoire runs in French. That gap is a competitive advantage for people who look past it.

Abidjan is West Africa's economic capital in a way that Accra and Lagos compete for but Abidjan quietly holds. The port is the busiest in West Africa. The banking sector is regional. The city has the infrastructure density of a city operating at a different tier than its neighbors, with the Plateau business district offering a skyline that surprises most first-time visitors.

**The Visa Pathway**

Côte d'Ivoire does not have a dedicated retirement visa. The pathway for long-term residents is a Carte de Séjour obtained through the Office National de l'État Civil et de l'Identification. Contact the Ivorian Embassy or consulate for current documentation requirements and fees. Processing runs through Abidjan regardless of where you apply.

**Abidjan Neighborhoods**

Abidjan is a city of distinct communes on a lagoon system that separates different urban areas.

- **Cocody:** The upscale residential commune. The Riviera neighborhoods (Riviera 2, Riviera 3) are where international residents and diplomats live. Good private schools, international restaurants, embassy infrastructure. This is the Accra East Legon equivalent.
- **Marcory / Bietry:** More mixed but increasingly popular with international residents looking for more authentic city texture at lower cost.
- **Plateau:** The central business district. Good for those who want to be close to the economic activity.

**Healthcare**

Abidjan's private healthcare sector is among the strongest in Francophone West Africa. Polyclinique Sainte Anne-Marie and the Clinique Générale d'Abidjan are well-regarded private facilities. For major procedures, medical evacuation to France (the former colonial power, still closely connected medically) is well-established and fast. Abidjan has direct flights to Paris.

**What Abidjan Does for Your Daily Life**

The food is outstanding. Ivorian cuisine (attiéké, kedjenou, aloko) anchors daily eating and restaurant culture is strong. The music scene is rooted in Zouglou and Afrobeats, with Abidjan claiming legitimate parentage in the global Afrobeats wave. The city runs at an urban pace with serious economic energy underneath it.

For Francophone African diaspora retirees, Abidjan is what Accra is for Anglophone diaspora: the hub city of the region with the community infrastructure to match.

Kenya gets on this list primarily for one reason: **Diani Beach**.

Approximately 30 kilometers south of Mombasa, Diani Beach is one of the most beautiful stretches of coastline in Africa. White sand, warm Indian Ocean water, baobab trees lining the beach road. It is calm. It is accessible. It is the kind of place that changes what you think retirement can feel like.

**The Class G Retirement Permit**

Kenya has a formal retirement permit for people over 50 who can demonstrate a minimum monthly income of approximately $1,000 (verify current requirements with Kenya's Department of Immigration). The permit is 5-year renewable and allows you to live in Kenya without working. This is one of the more accessible retirement visas on the continent in terms of income threshold.

**Nairobi vs. the Coast**

For retirees who want city life, Nairobi's Karen and Runda suburbs offer a genuinely good quality of life: green, spacious, with international school infrastructure and the best private hospitals in East Africa (Aga Khan University Hospital, Nairobi Hospital).

For the retirement most people imagine when they think about Kenya, the coast is the answer. Diani Beach has a growing expat community, good internet infrastructure, and Mombasa accessible in an hour for hospital-level care. Nairobi is 45 minutes by Air Kenya or Fly540.

## What No Retirement Guide Will Tell You

**The first year is always harder than the research.**

Every piece of research you do before moving, including this one, is based on systems functioning normally. Things do not always function normally. The electricity goes out for 12 hours during the season you did not prepare for. The bank requires a document that was not on the official list. Your first landlord is not what your WhatsApp group said to expect.

None of this is a reason not to go. All of it is a reason to plan for friction, not just for cost.

The diaspora communities in Accra, Cape Town, Kigali, and Dakar exist partly to help new arrivals navigate this. They have the lived-experience knowledge that no guide captures. Find them before you arrive. They will tell you which neighborhood the guide missed, which doctor speaks English and takes international insurance, which bank actually processes international transfers without a problem.

**On healthcare: be honest with yourself.**

If you have a complex chronic condition, diabetes requiring specialist management, or a cardiac history, the countries with the strongest healthcare infrastructure move up the list regardless of cost. That means South Africa (Cape Town or Johannesburg) for the best care, with Botswana and Kenya offering accessible evacuation routes to South African hospitals.

Get medical evacuation insurance. It costs $200-$400/year and it is worth every cent.

**On the exchange rate.**

Three of the strongest-performing currencies for US dollar retirees in Africa right now are the South African rand, the Namibian dollar (pegged to the rand), and the Rwandan franc. Your Social Security or pension income in USD buys more in these economies than it did five years ago.

The CFA franc (Senegal, Côte d'Ivoire) is pegged to the euro: stable but not discounted. The Ghanaian cedi has been volatile. The Kenyan shilling has been pressured.

None of this means avoid those countries. It means understand what your fixed income actually buys and build in a buffer.

**On dual citizenship.**

If you are of African heritage and you have not looked into citizenship by descent in your ancestral country, you should. Ghana, Nigeria, Senegal, Sierra Leone, and several other countries have pathways. Our [Citizenship Directory](/directories/citizenship) covers seven countries with verified program details. Holding African citizenship alongside your US or UK passport does not close any doors. It opens them.

**A note on Bitcoin.**

For retirees managing international money transfers, the traditional banking system in several of these countries creates friction: wire delays, conversion fees, transfer limits. Lightning Network payments via apps like Bitnob work in Ghana, Kenya, Nigeria, and several other African markets for near-zero-fee peer-to-peer transfers. Machankura operates USSD-based Bitcoin access across nine African countries, no smartphone required. This is not financial advice. It is infrastructure that exists and that diaspora retirees managing income across borders are quietly using.
